Most specialist manufacturers have one. A page headed Industries, Sectors or Markets, with a grid of six to ten tiles: aerospace, automotive, medical, defence, energy, food and drink. Click a tile and there are usually two paragraphs about long experience in the sector, exacting standards and its unique challenges, followed by a contact button.

The page exists for a sensible reason. The business genuinely serves those sectors and wants buyers to know. The trouble is that it answers a question the buyer was not really asking.

## Buyers search with a problem, not a sector

An engineer at a medical device company rarely sets out looking for "a supplier to the medical industry". They are looking for someone who can produce a thin stainless steel part without burrs, or line a chemical storage bund that will resist a particular acid, or supply a treatment chemical compatible with an existing closed-loop system. Their sector shapes the requirements, but the search starts with the part, the process or the failure they are trying to prevent.

So a sector page tells them you are in roughly the right neighbourhood. It does not tell them whether you have solved their problem before, under the constraints they care about. That second question is the one that decides whether they get in touch, and on most sector pages it is left for the sales conversation.

That matters because much of the evaluation happens before any conversation. Gartner research found that buyers in complex B2B purchases spent only around 17% of their buying time meeting suppliers, and that time was shared across every supplier under consideration. The study dates from 2018 and proportions will vary by market, but the principle holds for most technical purchases: a lot of shortlisting is done by people reading your website without you in the room. If your site cannot show suitability, a competitor's might.

## What an application page does differently

An application page is organised around a specific use: what the buyer is trying to make, protect, treat or achieve. Instead of "Aerospace", it might be "Precision shims for aerospace assemblies" or "Chemical-resistant linings for secondary containment".

The difference is not just the label. A good application page can be specific in ways a sector page cannot:

- what the requirement usually involves, such as tolerances, materials, standards or operating conditions
- where the common failure points are, and how your process avoids them
- the limits: thicknesses, volumes or conditions you do not cover
- evidence that you have done this before, such as a project summary, test data or an accreditation that actually applies
- what the buyer should send you to get a useful answer, and what happens next

Most of these help the engineer evaluating you, and several help the person who has to justify the choice internally. The limits deserve a particular mention. Saying what falls outside your range can feel commercially risky, but it saves both sides a wasted enquiry and makes the claims you do make more believable. This is the same thinking behind a good [landing page](/services#landing-pages): one clear purpose, the evidence that supports it and an obvious next step.

Application pages also tend to serve [search](/services#seo) better. The phrases buyers use while researching a specific job are far more likely to match a page about that job than a page that mentions their sector in passing.

## Which applications deserve a page

The obvious mistake is to swap one grid for a bigger one: forty application pages produced in a fortnight, each a lightly reworded template. Google's spam policies describe doorway abuse as pages created to rank for similar searches that are less useful than the destination they lead to, and list substantially similar pages among the examples. Even setting search engines aside, a thin page that says what nine others say does nothing for a buyer.

It is better to be selective. Before an application earns its own page, ask four questions.

1. **Is it commercially worth winning?** Consider volume, margin and fit with your capacity. A page should support work you want more of, not every job you have ever done.
2. **Can you prove it?** If you cannot point to projects, data, approvals or people who have done it, the page will be all assertion. Wait until you can.
3. **Do buyers ask different questions?** If two applications share the same requirements, objections and next steps, one page probably serves both. Separate pages are justified when the buyer's concerns genuinely differ.
4. **Do people describe it in a recognisable way?** Search data helps where you have it, but so does asking sales what customers call the job in their first email. If nobody describes it that way, it may be a heading rather than a page.

Applications that pass all four usually make a short list. That is the point. Three strong pages that each answer a real buyer's questions are worth more than thirty that answer none.

## Keep the sector page, but give it a smaller job

None of this means deleting the industries page. For some readers, particularly procurement teams running supplier assessments, confirming sector experience and the relevant accreditations is a genuine early filter. The sector page can do that well if it stops trying to persuade on its own and becomes a route into the application pages: for each sector, the applications you handle, the standards that apply and a link to the evidence.

That turns it from a list of claims into navigation, which is a job it can actually do.

## Where to start

If you suspect your industries pages are underperforming, compare them with your enquiries. Take the last twenty or thirty quote requests that became real opportunities and note what each was for, in the customer's own words. The work will often cluster around a handful of applications, and few of those clusters will have a page that speaks to them directly.

Those clusters are your shortlist. Build a page for the strongest one first, record the enquiries it produces (source, page and whether they reach quote), and use what you learn before writing the next. If enquiries are not yet tracked that way, [start with measurement](/insights/why-b2b-websites-dont-generate-enquiries).

Be honest, too, about what an application page cannot fix. If the pricing, lead times or capability are wrong for a buyer, a clearer page will simply help them reach that conclusion sooner. That is still useful, but it is not a website problem.
